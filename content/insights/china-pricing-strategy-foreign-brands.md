## China pricing strategy is a constraint problem, not a markup

**A China pricing strategy is the method a foreign brand uses to set retail prices for the Chinese market so that the landed-cost floor, the channel ladder and the regulatory price points all clear at the same time.** Most brands get this backwards: they convert a home-market price, check that it looks reasonable, and discover the problems later — the platform fees eat the margin, the cosmetics formula crosses a consumption-tax threshold nobody flagged, and the flagship SKU prices itself out of the cross-border channel entirely. Pricing for China is better treated as three simultaneous constraints. The floor comes from your import route's cost stack. The band comes from the channels between you and the buyer. And the cliffs come from tax and quota thresholds that change treatment at specific price points.

## Start from the landed-cost floor of the route you will actually ship

**The landed-cost floor is the all-in cost of putting one unit on a Chinese shelf or into one bonded parcel, and the import route moves that floor more than the product itself does.** The same SKU has two different floors. On the cross-border route within the RMB 5,000 and RMB 26,000 limits, duty is zero and value-added and consumption taxes are charged at 70 percent of the statutory amount — about 9.1 percent all-in for a typical 13 percent VAT good ([Cai Guan Shui 49 of 2018](https://gss.mof.gov.cn/gzdt/zhengcefabu/201811/t20181129_3079073.htm)). On general trade, duty, consumption tax and full import VAT all apply, and freight and insurance sit inside the customs value. If you have not yet walked this stack line by line, [our landed-cost walkthrough](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/china-import-taxes-landed-cost.html) gives the formulas. Price below your floor and every sale loses money at full price; the floor is not negotiable, the price point is.

## The channel ladder sets the retail price band

**A channel ladder is the stack of commissions, fees and margins that comes off the retail price between your landed cost and the money you keep.** On a cross-border platform store, the visible layers are modest: Tmall Global publishes a commission of 2 to 5 percent by category plus roughly 1 percent in cross-border payment processing, against a RMB 50,000 deposit and an annual fee of RMB 30,000 to 60,000 that does not return ([Tmall Global merchant FAQ](https://www.tmall.hk)). Offline the ladder deepens — national importer, regional distributor, retail door — each layer taking margin, which is why the same product often retails far higher in physical stores. Platform charges are also regulated: SAMR's fee-compliance guide, effective July 2025, requires platforms to publish fee rules and bans unreasonable deposits and forced service bundles ([SAMR platform fee compliance guide](https://www.samr.gov.cn/xw/zj/art/2025/art_c35be0a9ae514fd9b37bfd3620b678c1.html)). Map every layer before setting the price; see [our channel-margin breakdown](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/china-distribution-margin-by-channel.html).

## Two price cliffs should shape your price points

**A price cliff is a threshold in China's tax or quota rules where moving the retail price a few yuan changes the legal or tax treatment of the entire order.** The first cliff is the high-end cosmetics consumption tax: it applies at 15 percent once the dutiable unit value reaches RMB 10 per millilitre or gram, or RMB 15 per piece — and on the cross-border route that takes the effective rate from about 9.1 percent to about 23.1 percent. Brands re-engineer pack sizes and unit prices to stay under it. The second cliff is the cross-border quota itself: a single transaction above RMB 5,000 loses the preferential treatment entirely, so a premium SKU priced above the limit needs a split order, a different route, or a general-trade structure with registration. Both cliffs sit inside the same policy ([Cai Guan Shui 49 of 2018](https://gss.mof.gov.cn/gzdt/zhengcefabu/201811/t20181129_3079073.htm)); scan for them before you benchmark competitors, not after.

## Price the route, not the product

**Route pricing means keeping two deliberate price architectures — one for the cross-border channel and one for general trade — instead of averaging a single number across both.** The cross-border route's tax floor is structurally lower, so the same product can retail lower there and still hold margin; that is a feature of the regime, not a discount. General trade carries duty, possible consumption tax, full import VAT, plus registration and labelling costs, so its floor is higher and its price should be too. Two disciplines keep the pair honest. First, compute each floor independently and never let one channel's price be set by the other's. Second, watch the divergence: a gap wide enough to tempt grey-market arbitrage or frustrate distributors is a channel-design problem, and it is solved with territory and pricing clauses, not with pretending the two routes cost the same.

## Display prices compliantly, or the price is a liability

**Price display in China is regulated disclosure, not a marketing choice.** The E-Commerce Law requires operators to disclose goods information fully, truthfully, accurately and timely, and prohibits fabricated transactions and invented reviews ([E-Commerce Law, Article 17](http://www.npc.gov.cn/zgrdw/npc/xinwen/lfgz/2018-09/18/content_2061389.htm)) — invented "original prices" for discount framing sit squarely against it. Cross-border adds a second duty: the seller must present the risk notice and give the buyer access to the Chinese electronic label, with the consumer's confirmation before the order is placed ([Shang Cai Fa 486 of 2018](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2018-12/31/content_5437823.htm)). And if your product page cites test results or "clinically proven" claims to justify a premium, the Advertising Law requires cited data to carry its source and validity period. A price built on a non-compliant claim is not a premium; it is a pending enforcement case.

## Validate the price before you commit it

**A China price is validated when real buyers pay it at target repeat rates, and a bonded pilot is the cheapest instrument that produces that evidence.** Search data and competitor shelves can bracket a price band, but they cannot tell you whether your brand carries the premium you assume. The sequence that works: run the SKU at full price through a 1210 bonded pilot, hold the price for a full season, and watch sell-through and repeat rate rather than headline volume ([the commitment-ladder method](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/china-market-entry-test-before-commit.html)). Correct for the sample's skew — cross-border buyers concentrate in wealthier coastal provinces, so a price that clears in Shanghai says little about Chengdu offline ([regional demand differences](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/china-market-entry-regional-demand.html)). Write the acceptance thresholds down before launch.
