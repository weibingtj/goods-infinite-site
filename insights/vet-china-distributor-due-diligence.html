How to Vet a China Distributor: The Checks, the Red Flags and the Contract Clauses

A Chinese distributor can put your product in front of millions of buyers — or register your trademark before you do, gray-import your own stock back at you, and quietly replace you once the category is proven. Here is the due-diligence sequence that catches the second kind: four free official registries, the first-to-file trademark trap, the clauses Chinese law will and will not enforce for you, and the contract terms that survive a dispute.

Published 2026-10-23 · Last updated 2026-10-26 · By Bing Wei, China Market Entry & Cross-Border Commerce Specialist

Definition: what "vetting a China distributor" actually means

Vetting a China distributor means verifying, against official Chinese government registers and before you sign anything, that the local company you are about to hand your brand, your stock and your pricing to is (a) the entity it claims to be, (b) legally able to do the import and distribution it is promising, and (c) not in a position to appropriate your trademark, your registration certificates or your customers.

It is not a credit report and it is not a reference call. Those are useful, but they answer a softer question. The hard question is narrower and more actionable: what does the Chinese government's own public record say about this company, and does my contract survive the answer?

That distinction matters because of scale. As of the end of May 2025 there were 185 million private-sector business entities registered in China, 96.76% of all market entities, including more than 58 million private enterprises and 127 million individual businesses (State Administration for Market Regulation data reported by People's Daily, 28 June 2025). "We are a large Chinese distributor" is a statement that costs nothing to make and is almost impossible to disprove over email. It takes about an hour to disprove properly.

Why this is a legal problem, not a commercial one

Most foreign brands approach distributor selection commercially — margin, coverage, enthusiasm. The failures that actually destroy a China launch are legal and procedural:

  1. The trademark was filed by the distributor first. China is first-to-file: under Art. 31 of the Trademark Law, where two parties apply for identical or similar marks on identical or similar goods, the earlier application is preliminarily approved and published. If your distributor filed your brand in China before you did, you are the one bringing an objection.
  2. The registrations sit in the distributor's name. For regulated categories — food, cosmetics, medical devices, certain electrical goods — the approvals are held by a Chinese entity. If that entity is the distributor's, termination does not end the relationship; it transfers the leverage.
  3. The "exclusive for China" grant was broader than intended. A single exclusive clause can lock a brand out of channels, platforms and city tiers it never meant to give away, with no performance obligation attached.
  4. The contract contains terms Chinese law will not enforce the way you expect. Resale price maintenance is the classic example.
  5. The counterparty on the signature page is not the counterparty you checked. Shell entities with near-identical names are common. The 18-character Unified Social Credit Code is what disambiguates them.

Step 1 — Verify the entity on the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System

Registry: 国家企业信用信息公示系统 (National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System), operated by the State Administration for Market Regulation. Free, public, no payment and no login required for basic queries (Beijing Municipal Administration for Market Regulation).

Search by the exact Chinese company name or, better, by the Unified Social Credit Code (统一社会信用代码) — the 18-character identifier on the business licence. Ask for the licence scan up front; a company that will not send one has told you what you need to know.

What the record gives you:

| Field | What you are checking | Why it matters | |---|---|---| | Registration status | 存续 / 吊销 / 注销 | A company that has been revoked or cancelled and is still trading is a serious problem, not a technicality | | Unified Social Credit Code | 18 characters, matches the contract and the invoice | Prevents signature-page substitution | | Legal representative (法定代表人) | Name to cross-check against court records | Individual-level enforcement records attach to the person, not just the company | | Business scope (经营范围) | Does the scope actually cover import, distribution, or the specific regulated category? | A trading company cannot lawfully do what its licence does not cover | | Registered capital, paid-in capital, shareholders | Who actually owns and controls the entity | Identifies affiliates that may strip value | | Administrative licences | Food, cosmetics, medical device, CCC-scope licences where applicable | Tells you whether it can hold your product legally | | Administrative penalties | Prior enforcement history | Repeated penalties in your category are predictive | | Annual reports (年报) | Filed each year, 1 January – 30 June | Gaps are a live signal, not a filing oversight | | Abnormal operation list (经营异常名录) | Four statutory grounds under Art. 4 of the Measures for the Administration of the List of Enterprises with Abnormal Operations (formerly SAIC Order No. 68, as amended) — failure to publish an annual report, failure to publish required information after being ordered to, failure to register a name change, and being unreachable at the registered address | "Unreachable at the registered address" is the single most useful free red flag in the whole system | | Serious illegal and untrustworthy list (严重违法失信名单) | Listed or not | Brings joint punishment across government agencies |

Two of these deserve emphasis. Unreachable at the registered address means the market regulator itself could not find the company where it says it is. And the serious illegal and untrustworthy list has recently been rebuilt: SAMR Order No. 128 replaced the 2021 rules and took effect 15 July 2026, adding commercial bribery, serious intellectual-property infringement including bad-faith trademark filings that harm the public interest, falsified public information, and evading enforcement of an administrative penalty or ruling as independent grounds for listing (SAMR; summary by Dazhou Municipal Administration for Market Regulation).

Step 2 — Verify customs standing, not "import and export rights"

Ask a candidate for its customs record and then check it yourself on the China Customs Enterprise Import and Export Credit Information Publicity Platform (中国海关企业进出口信用信息公示平台). Customs itself has confirmed this is the channel for checking whether a company is an AEO enterprise (General Administration of Customs, "Customs Policy Direct" No. 4, 13 January 2026).

The legal basis is the Measures of the Customs of the PRC for the Credit Administration of Registered and Filed Enterprises (GACC Order No. 251, in force 1 November 2021), which requires customs to publish: enterprise registration and filing information; the result of customs credit certification or determination; administrative licensing information; administrative penalties; and joint incentive / joint punishment information (Art. 11). The published credit tier is the payoff:

| Credit status | Published effect under GACC Order No. 251 | |---|---| | Advanced certified enterprise (AEO) | Average inspection rate on imports and exports below 20% of the average rate applied to enterprises under ordinary management; priority clearance; reduced audit frequency; possible guarantee exemption; dedicated customs coordinator (Art. 30) | | Ordinary management | Standard measures | | Dishonest enterprise (失信企业) | Inspection rate of 80% or above; full guarantee for processing trade; increased audit and inspection frequency (Art. 31) |

A distributor on the customs dishonest list will have four out of five shipments inspected. If that distributor is holding your inventory, the delay lands on you, not on them.

< 20% vs ≥ 80%Average import/export inspection rate for a GACC advanced-certified (AEO) enterprise — below 20% of the rate applied to ordinary enterprises — versus an inspection rate of 80% or above for an enterprise customs has determined to be dishonest. Same goods, same declaration, two completely different clearance profiles.Source: GACC Order No. 251, Arts. 30 and 31

Two clarifications that save time:

  • "Import and export rights" is nearly meaningless as a claim now. On 30 December 2022 the NPC Standing Committee deleted Art. 9 of the Foreign Trade Law; MOFCOM stopped processing foreign-trade operator registrations the same day, and companies engaged in import and export obtain the status automatically (MOFCOM, Foreign Trade Department; MOFCOM public Q&A).
  • Customs registration is still required. Art. 11 of the Customs Law requires consignees of imported goods and customs brokers to be registered with customs before handling customs formalities; this is a separate requirement that survived the 2022 trade-law change. This is the document to ask for.

Step 3 — Check whether the company and its legal representative are being enforced against

Registry: 中国执行信息公开网 (China Enforcement Information Publicity Website) of the Supreme People's Court. Search the company name and, separately, the legal representative's name.

Look for: 被执行人 (person subject to enforcement), 失信被执行人 (dishonest person subject to enforcement — the "laolai" list, which carries consumption and travel restrictions), 限制消费令 (consumption restriction orders) and termination-of-case records.

The volume here is not marginal. At a Supreme People's Court press conference on 26 January 2026, the court reported that in 2025 2.3398 million person-times were newly added to the dishonest list, 2.6696 million person-times exited through credit repair, and 18.6518 million persons subject to enforcement performed their obligations under the pressure of enforcement sanctions (People's Daily, 27 January 2026). Checking costs five minutes. Not checking is how brands end up holding unpaid invoices from a counterparty that already has twenty of them.

Also worth searching: China Judgments Online (wenshu.court.gov.cn) for prior contract disputes, and Credit China (www.creditchina.gov.cn) for cross-agency credit records.

Step 4 — Check your own trademark status before you appoint anyone

This step is about you, not the distributor, and it is the one most often skipped.

Under the Trademark Law (as amended in 2019):

  • Art. 31 — first to file. Where two or more applicants file identical or similar marks for identical or similar goods, the earlier application is preliminarily approved and published.
  • Art. 4 — bad faith. "Bad-faith trademark registration applications not intended for use shall be rejected."
  • Art. 32 — prior rights and pre-emptive filing. No application may damage another party's existing prior rights, nor may a party pre-emptively register by improper means a mark already in use by another party that has acquired a certain degree of influence.
  • Art. 15 — the distributor case, stated explicitly. Where an agent or representative registers the trademark of its principal in its own name without authorisation, the registration must be refused and its use prohibited if the principal objects. Art. 15(2) extends the same outcome to an applicant who, by virtue of a contract, business dealings or other relationship, knew of the other party's earlier-used unregistered mark on identical or similar goods.
  • Art. 33 — objection window. An objection against a preliminarily approved mark must be filed within three months of publication.
  • Art. 68(4) — administrative penalty. Bad-faith filings are punishable by warning and fine according to the circumstances.

The 2019 implementing rules — Provisions on Regulating Trademark Application and Registration (SAMR Order No. 17) — spell out in Art. 3 that an agent or representative registering the principal's mark without authorisation, and a party that registers a mark knowing of another's prior use through contract or business dealings, are both bad-faith filings; Art. 5 requires the registry to reject them, and Art. 12 sets administrative fines of up to three times illegal gain capped at RMB 30,000, or up to RMB 10,000 where there is no illegal gain.

3 monthsThe objection window against a preliminarily approved Chinese trademark application, running from publication (Trademark Law Art. 33). Miss it and the fight moves to invalidation, which is slower and more expensive. Search your own brand on the China Trademark Office site before appointing a distributor — and periodically after.Source: Trademark Law of the PRC (2019 amendment), Arts. 15, 31, 32, 33, 68

Action: search your brand in Latin script, in Chinese characters, and in any plausible transliteration on the China National Intellectual Property Administration trademark portal, before you sign. Then monitor. Distributor squatting is not usually a pre-planned heist; it is usually a hedge taken after the relationship sours.

Step 5 — Three clauses Chinese law will not give you

These come up in almost every negotiation, and in each case the foreign brand's instinct is wrong.

Resale price maintenance

Art. 18 of the Anti-Monopoly Law (as amended 2022) prohibits agreements between an operator and its counterparty that fix the price for resale to third parties or set a minimum resale price. The agreement is not prohibited if the operator can prove it has no effect of excluding or restricting competition, and there is a safe harbour where the operator can prove its market share in the relevant market is below the threshold set by the enforcement authority and other conditions are met (NPC Standing Committee decision amending the Anti-Monopoly Law, 24 June 2022).

The thresholds were made operational by the 2025 amendment to the Provisions on Prohibiting Monopoly Agreements (SAMR Order No. 111, in force 1 February 2026): for price-related vertical agreements both parties' market shares must be below 5% in every year of the agreement and the annual turnover of the contract goods below RMB 100 million; for non-price vertical agreements (territory, customer restrictions) the threshold is 15% market share with no turnover condition — and both parties must qualify, every year (Putian Municipal Administration for Market Regulation summary of SAMR Order No. 111).

Practical drafting: a maximum resale price or a recommended retail price, combined with control over brand presentation, channel listing and promotional mechanics. Do not build a mechanism for detecting and punishing price-cutting.

Commercial bribery conducted "for" you

Art. 7 of the Anti-Unfair Competition Law prohibits an operator from bribing, by money or other means, the counterparty's personnel, a unit or individual entrusted by the counterparty, or a unit or individual able to influence the transaction through authority or influence, in order to obtain a transaction opportunity or competitive advantage. Critically, Art. 7(3) provides that bribery by an operator's personnel is deemed to be the act of the operator — unless the operator proves the conduct was unrelated to obtaining business for it. Penalties under Art. 19 run from RMB 100,000 to RMB 3 million, confiscation of illegal gain, and revocation of the business licence in serious cases.

A distributor that buys shelf space or buyer favour in your brand's name is creating exposure you may end up sharing. Put an anti-bribery covenant in the agreement, require it to flow down to the distributor's own sales staff, and give yourself an audit and termination right.

"Exclusive for China" without performance

There is no legal obstacle to exclusivity, but a broad grant with no objective performance test is a one-way option written against you. If you grant exclusivity, tie it to measurable commitments — named channels, minimum purchase volumes, listing obligations, marketing spend — tested at defined intervals with a cure period and automatic conversion to non-exclusive on failure.

Step 6 — Red flags and what they usually mean

These are patterns, not accusations. Each one alone may have an innocent explanation; two or more together is a decision.

| Signal | What it usually means | |---|---| | Company declines to send a business licence scan, or the Unified Social Credit Code on the contract differs from the one on the licence | You may be contracting with a different entity than the one you are negotiating with | | Registered address is "unreachable" on gsxt.gov.cn | The regulator could not find them where they say they are | | Registered capital is very large, paid-in is zero or nominal, and the entity was incorporated recently | A vehicle, not a business | | Business scope does not cover your regulated category, or covers it only by a generic "sales of…" formula | It cannot hold your product lawfully, or will subcontract the licence holding to someone you have not vetted | | Presses hard for exclusivity and a long term before any market test | It is buying optionality at your cost | | Wants the product registration certificate in its own name as a condition of proceeding | Building termination leverage | | Asks you not to register your trademark because "we will handle it" | The single highest-risk sentence in China distribution | | Payment to a personal account, or to a different company's account than the one on the contract | Either tax structuring or fraud; both are your problem | | Customs credit status is "dishonest" or AEO status claimed but not appearing on credit.customs.gov.cn | Your inventory will sit | | Legal representative appears on the enforcement list | The control person is already constrained | | Claims "foreign trade operator registration" as a credential | No longer issued since 30 December 2022 — a stale claim suggests stale advice generally |

Step 7 — Insure the receivable

Commercial prevention is structural; insurance is the backstop. China Export & Credit Insurance Corporation (Sinosure) is China's policy export credit insurer. Its short-term cover responds to commercial risks including buyer insolvency, buyer default on payment obligations, buyer rejection of goods, and buyer breach causing early termination, as well as defined political risks (Sinosure short-term specific-contract policy, 2025 version).

Note the fit question honestly: Sinosure's export policies insure Chinese-registered exporters. If you sell to a Chinese distributor from an overseas entity, the practical routes are your own market credit insurer, your export credit agency, or structuring the sale through a Hong Kong or mainland trading arm. What Sinosure usefully proves is that buyer default, insolvency and rejection are insurable, priced risks — which means the right response to a distributor that refuses any third-party check is not to relax the check, but to shorten the credit.

The twelve-point pre-signature checklist

  1. Obtain the business licence and record the 18-character Unified Social Credit Code.
  2. Search gsxt.gov.cn using that code. Read status, business scope, legal representative, licences, penalties, annual reports, abnormal-operation and serious-illegal-and-untrustworthy listings.
  3. Search credit.customs.gov.cn for customs credit status. Treat "dishonest" as disqualifying for anyone holding your inventory.
  4. Search zxgk.court.gov.cn for the company and the legal representative; read wenshu.court.gov.cn for prior contract disputes.
  5. Search your own brand on the CNIPA trademark portal in Latin script, Chinese characters and likely transliterations. If anything is filed, get advice before signing.
  6. Confirm the signatory's authority — the licence's legal representative, or a validly sealed power of attorney.
  7. Map the ownership chain. Shareholders, affiliates, and other entities under the same controlling person.
  8. Confirm the business scope covers the regulated category and that any required licence is held by an entity you have also vetted.
  9. Decide who holds the product registrations. If it must be a Chinese entity, prefer your own, or a custodial arrangement with transfer triggers — not the distributor's by default.
  10. Draft the price clause as a maximum or recommendation, not a minimum, and check the safe-harbour thresholds.
  11. Insert an anti-bribery covenant with flow-down to the distributor's staff, plus audit and termination rights.
  12. Stage the credit. Prepayment or a small first order; escalate limits only against demonstrated payment history; no open account on the strength of a relationship.

What we deliberately do not assert

This article stays with what we can point to in official text. Three things are commonly claimed in vendor and law-firm content that we will not repeat:

  • We do not give a cost or timeline for Chinese trademark registration. No official schedule is published and third-party figures vary widely.
  • We do not describe any named company's distributor dispute. We have no official source for the specifics of any particular case.

FAQ

How can I check whether a Chinese company is legitimate before signing a distribution agreement? Start with the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System (gsxt.gov.cn), run by the State Administration for Market Regulation. Searching the exact Chinese company name or the 18-character Unified Social Credit Code returns registration status, business scope, legal representative, registered capital, administrative licences, administrative penalties, annual reports, and any listing on the abnormal-operation list or the serious-illegal-and-untrustworthy list. It is free and open to the public. Then cross-check customs credit at credit.customs.gov.cn and court enforcement records at zxgk.court.gov.cn.

Can my China distributor register my trademark in China and block me? It can try, and it sometimes succeeds if you have not filed first. China is a first-to-file system under Art. 31 of the Trademark Law. But Art. 15 gives you a direct defence: an agent or representative that registers the principal's trademark in its own name without authorisation must be refused registration and is prohibited from using it, and Art. 15(2) extends that to parties who knew of your earlier-used mark through a contract, business dealings or other relationship. File your own Chinese trademark before you appoint any distributor.

Is "we have import and export rights" a meaningful claim from a Chinese distributor? Much less than it used to be. On 30 December 2022 the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress deleted Art. 9 of the Foreign Trade Law, and MOFCOM stopped issuing foreign-trade operator registrations the same day — companies now obtain foreign-trade status automatically. What still matters is customs registration: under Art. 11 of the Customs Law, a consignee of imported goods must be registered with customs to file declarations. Ask for the customs registration record and check it, not for a trade-operator certificate nobody issues any more.

Can I fix the resale price my China distributor charges? Be careful. Art. 18 of the Anti-Monopoly Law (as amended in 2022) prohibits agreements that fix the price for resale to third parties or set a minimum resale price. An agreement is not prohibited if you can show it has no effect of excluding or restricting competition, and a safe harbour applies where both parties' market shares and the contract turnover stay below the thresholds set by the enforcement authority. Practically: set a maximum resale price or a recommended price, control brand and channel, and do not write an enforceable minimum retail price into the contract.

What should a China distribution agreement say about termination and inventory? Define the territory and channels precisely rather than granting "China" in the abstract; set objective performance thresholds with a cure period; require the distributor to transfer or cancel any e-commerce storefront and platform accounts it opened in your brand name; deal explicitly with remaining inventory and with who owns the customer data; and provide for the return of product registration certificates, which in many regulated categories are held by a local entity and are the distributor's real leverage over you.

How do I protect against non-payment by a Chinese distributor? Check the company and its legal representative against the Supreme People's Court enforcement database before you extend credit, start with prepayment or a small first order, keep title until payment where the contract allows it, escalate credit only against actual payment history, and consider export credit insurance — China Export & Credit Insurance Corporation (Sinosure), the state policy insurer, writes cover against buyer insolvency, protracted default and rejection of goods.
