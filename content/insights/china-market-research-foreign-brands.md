## What China market research is for a foreign brand

China market research for foreign brands is the systematic process of estimating demand for a specific product at a specific price in mainland China, using official statistics, platform-level signals and low-risk commercial tests, before committing capital to distribution, entities or inventory. It is not the same as buying a category report: a report can tell you that China's total retail sales of consumer goods reached about RMB 47.1 trillion (roughly US$6.6 trillion) in 2023 ([National Bureau of Statistics](https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/)), but it cannot tell you whether *your* product clears Chinese customs smoothly, whether your price point survives the platform fee stack, or whether Chinese consumers trust your claims. This playbook sets out the four research layers that actually produce decision-grade evidence, in the order a foreign brand should execute them.

## Why desk research alone fails before entering the China market

Category-level statistics answer the wrong question for a single brand. China had over 1.1 billion internet users as of December 2024 ([CNNIC](https://www.cnnic.net.cn)), but the question that decides your launch is narrower: of the consumers who search for your category, how many accept an imported brand at your price, and on which platform do they buy? Desk research systematically overstates opportunity because it counts category demand without the three filters that kill foreign products in practice — regulatory fit (is your product on the cross-border positive list and registered where required), price fit (the landed price after duty, platform commission and KOL commissions), and claim fit (Chinese advertising law is far stricter on efficacy language than EU or US rules, under [SAMR](https://www.samr.gov.cn) enforcement).

The practical consequence: treat every desk-research number as a filter that can produce a "no", never as evidence that produces a "yes". Only first-party sales data from a real commercial test can justify the "yes".

## Layer 1 — size the category with official data

Start with the sources that publish verifiable, dated numbers:

- **National Bureau of Statistics** — total retail sales, urban/rural income split, and per-capita consumption expenditure by category. This frames which city tiers can afford your price point ([NBS](https://www.stats.gov.cn/english/)).
- **General Administration of Customs (GACC)** — import volumes for your HS code and cross-border e-commerce trade values. Import volume tells you whether an official import channel for your category already exists at scale ([GACC](https://english.customs.gov.cn)).
- **CNNIC statistical reports** — platform user bases and demographics, published twice yearly. Use these to size the audience on Xiaohongshu, Douyin and WeChat before allocating marketing budget ([CNNIC](https://www.cnnic.net.cn)).
- **MOFCOM** — cross-border e-commerce policy documents and the retail import positive list, which determines whether your product can enter through the fast cross-border route at all ([MOFCOM](https://www.mofcom.gov.cn)).

## Layer 2 — read the platform signals consumers actually generate

Official statistics lag; platform signals are real-time and free. Four checks take a competent researcher under two weeks:

1. **Search demand.** Run your category's Chinese-language terms through Baidu Index to see search volume and seasonality. If no one is searching your category's Chinese name, either your translation is wrong or demand is education-dependent — both are findings.
2. **Xiaohongshu saturation.** Search your category on Xiaohongshu (RED) and count how many imported competitors are seeding content, at what engagement, and with what claims. Xiaohongshu is where Chinese consumers research imported goods before buying, so its competitive density is a direct demand proxy.
3. **Competitor price ladders.** On Tmall Global and JD Worldwide, record the top 10 imported competitors' prices, promotional prices, and review counts. This produces your price corridor before you have designed a single label.
4. **Review mining.** Read one- and two-star reviews of the closest imported competitors on Douyin and Tmall. Chinese consumers' complaints — packaging, shelf life, sweetness, authenticity doubts — are your free product-localisation research.

## Layer 3 — test price and claims before you ship inventory

Between desk research and a full launch sits a cheap middle layer most brands skip. Three options, roughly in cost order:

- **Content-only test.** Run Xiaohongshu seeding and Douyin content for your product with a clear "coming soon" position, and measure save rates, comment questions and follow-through to a waitlist. Cost is a few thousand dollars in content and micro-influencer fees; it tests claim resonance without any customs exposure.
- **Daigou or small-parcel route.** Where your category permits, a modest reseller flow tests real willingness to pay at real prices. Limited in scale and not a compliant long-term channel, but it generates genuine transaction data.
- **1210 bonded pilot.** The definitive test: stock one bonded pallet, open a cross-border store on Tmall Global or Douyin, and sell for a full season. Tax is charged per order as units sell, so unsold stock incurs no import duty — the test is cheap to abandon. ## Layer 4 — the go/no-go decision, and what evidence it needs

Write the decision criteria down before the test starts, or every result becomes negotiable. A defensible threshold set looks like this:

- **Repeat rate.** At least 15–20% of buyers reorder within one season at your target price. One-off promotion sales prove nothing about the base.
- **Unit economics.** Contribution margin per unit is positive after platform commission (typically 5–8%), cross-border tax, bonded fulfilment, and a realistic marketing cost per order — not the launch-order cost, which is always flattering.
- **Compliance confirmation.** The product is on the cross-border positive list and any required registration pathway is identified and costed, including general-trade registration if offline retail is the goal. Our [registration compliance roadmap](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/china-product-registration-foreign-brands.html) maps the pathways by category.
- **Trademark security.** The China trademark (Latin and Chinese character marks) is filed before any distributor conversation, since China is first-to-file ([CNIPA](https://www.cnipa.gov.cn)).

Brands that meet these criteria proceed to scale with confidence; brands that miss them have spent thousands, not millions, to learn it. ## FAQ

**What is the best way for a foreign brand to research the China market before entering?** Combine three layers: official statistics (NBS, GACC, CNNIC) to size the category, platform signals (Baidu Index, Xiaohongshu, competitor price ladders) to read real demand, and a low-risk 1210 cross-border commercial test to generate first-party sales data. Desk research alone systematically overstates opportunity because it ignores regulatory, price and claims fit.

**Which official data sources are reliable for China market research?** The National Bureau of Statistics for retail and income data, GACC for import volumes and cross-border trade values, CNNIC for platform user statistics, and MOFCOM for cross-border policy and the positive list. All publish in English and cite their reporting periods.

**How much does it cost to test demand in China before committing?** A single-pallet 1210 cross-border pilot typically starts at US$8,000–20,000 including first inventory, bonded warehousing, import agency and platform setup, with marketing budget separate. Because tax is charged per order, unsold inventory carries no import duty.

**How long does a China demand-validation test take?** 4–8 weeks to set up once compliance is confirmed, then 8–12 weeks of selling to capture at least one full marketing cycle and one promotion peak before judging the result.

**What evidence should a brand have before signing a China distributor?** A filed China trademark, a confirmed compliance pathway, 3–6 months of cross-border sales showing repeat purchase at target price, and a mapped price ladder that leaves margin for the distributor's markup.
