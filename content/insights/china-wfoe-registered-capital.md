## China WFOE registered capital is a dated commitment, not a headline number

**China WFOE registered capital is the amount of capital the shareholders of a wholly foreign-owned enterprise commit to contribute, registered with the market regulator and recorded in the company's articles.** It is not a licence fee, not a working-capital estimate you can revise quietly, and not money that has to sit idle. Since 1 July 2024 it is also a dated promise: the revised Company Law caps the window for paying it in at five years from incorporation ([Company Law of the PRC, Article 47](https://www.gov.cn/yaowen/liebiao/202312/content_6923395.htm)). That turned a static registration field into a schedule with penalties attached — which is why a figure that looked unremarkable in 2022 can be a board problem in 2027.

Two concepts carry the subject. Subscribed capital is the number in the articles; paid-in capital is what has landed. Foreign-invested enterprises follow the same law through Article 31 of the Foreign Investment Law — no separate capital regime, only extra foreign-exchange steps on the way in.

## Every company wants a minimum, and there is no general one

**A minimum registered capital is a statutory floor below which a company cannot be registered at all, and in China today it survives only in specific licensed sectors rather than for foreign-invested trading or distribution companies generally.** The 2014 reform abolished blanket minimums, and the 2023 revision kept the subscribed system, deferring only to laws, administrative regulations and State Council decisions that set a floor for a named industry (Article 47, second paragraph). So the real floor for a foreign brand comes from three other places: the licence your activity requires, since a handful of sectors still publish thresholds; the regulator's power to reject an obviously abnormal figure; and the cash the entity must genuinely stand behind to trade.

That second point is the newest. State Council Order 784 gives the registration authority an explicit test: where contribution periods or registered capital are manifestly abnormal, it may weigh business scope, operating condition, shareholder means and asset scale, and order the company to adjust ([State Council Order 784, Article 3](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/content/202407/content_6960376.htm)). A four-figure capital on an entity that intends to import container loads is the pattern that test exists for. The minimum you choose is a judgement about credibility, not a loophole.

## The 2027 amendment deadline for entities set up before the reform

**The contribution period is the window written into the articles within which shareholders must pay in subscribed capital, and for older companies it is being forcibly shortened.** Order 784 sets the transition: where the remaining subscribed period would still exceed five years on 1 July 2027, the company must amend its articles to five years or less by 30 June 2027, putting the outermost paid-in date at 30 June 2032 (Order 784, Article 2). A carve-out exists where operations involve national or major public interest, but it needs a ministry or provincial government to make the case.

Compliance is not optional: a company that fails to adjust is ordered to correct and then flagged publicly on the national credit information system (Article 6), a flag visible to every bank and distributor doing diligence on the subsidiary.

## Capital can be called early if the company cannot pay its debts

**Acceleration is the right of a company or its creditors to demand unpaid subscribed capital ahead of the agreed schedule when the company cannot meet debts that have fallen due.** Article 54 makes it explicit: once the company cannot pay a matured debt, the company itself or a creditor holding that debt may require shareholders to pay in capital that is not yet due. Two neighbouring provisions complete the picture — Article 53 prohibits withdrawing contributed capital, and Articles 51 and 52 let the board issue a written call and, after a grace period of at least 60 days, strip a shareholder of the unpaid equity.

Registered capital is a contingent cash call, and for a cross-border shareholder the call must be met through the foreign-exchange route on short notice. If the number in the articles is one the parent could only fund after an approval cycle and a capital raise, the entity is one bad quarter away from a dispute with its subsidiary's creditors.

## How the money has to arrive

**Foreign exchange registration for foreign direct investment is the filing, made through a bank under State SAFE rules, that opens a company's capital account to foreign-currency or cross-border RMB contributions.** Capital must arrive through that account, against the registered amount and the articles, rather than being netted off against invoices. Contributions in kind are permitted but must be valued, transferred by title, and certified (Articles 48 and 55).

Plan for the administrative cycle, not just the wire. Every top-up means a fresh bank filing, a capital-account credit, accounting evidence and public disclosure — which is why a figure increased twice in two years costs more in friction than one sized correctly once. It is also why sizing belongs in the market-entry plan rather than the incorporation paperwork, alongside [the entry cost budget](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/china-market-entry-cost-budget.html) it has to fund.

## A very small number has its own cost: the 2× borrowing ceiling

**The cross-border financing ceiling is the maximum offshore borrowing a Chinese company may carry, calculated as net assets multiplied by a leverage ratio and the central bank's macro-prudential parameter.** Under the PBOC framework applied nationwide since 2017, the leverage ratio for enterprises is 2, so an ordinary company may borrow offshore up to twice its net assets, and foreign-invested enterprises may elect between the older investment-to-capital gap model and this framework ([SAFE policy explainer on Yin Fa 2017 No. 9](https://www.safe.gov.cn/hebei/file/file/20180424/c17bffbd6582428f8dfa9835e0ce1562.pdf)).

The consequence is direct and routinely missed. A brand that registers the smallest defensible capital, intending to fund the Chinese operation with intercompany loans, finds those loans capped by the number it minimised. Repeated capital increases reintroduce the five-year clock each time. Capital and debt are one decision and should be modelled together.

## Getting it back out: three doors and their tolls

**Repatriation is the transfer of a Chinese subsidiary's value back to its foreign shareholder, and there are only three lawful doors: dividends, an equity transfer, or liquidation.** Dividends require distributable profits after the statutory surplus reserve, and a non-resident corporate shareholder pays 10 percent withholding on the gross amount, reducible under an applicable tax treaty ([Enterprise Income Tax Law and STA guidance on the 10 percent rate](https://www.chinatax.gov.cn/chinatax/c102449/c5237520/content.html)). An equity transfer prices the company and sells the holding. Liquidation is the slow door: creditor notices, tax clearance, deregistration, and whatever remains.

Note what is missing from that list. Contributed capital cannot be returned on request; shrinking it needs a formal capital reduction with creditor notification. That asymmetry is the core argument for restraint — money paid in is cheap to send and expensive to recall; money left offshore can be deployed in weeks.

## Sizing the number for a first-entry import WFOE

**Capital sizing is the exercise of setting the subscribed amount so that it covers the entity's fixed costs for the first 18 to 24 months plus a regulatory buffer, without committing cash that cannot easily come back.** Three inputs do most of the work: the cost of the team and office the entity will carry, the licence route being funded, and whether the parent will fund growth through equity or intercompany debt. Then round up rather than down — both the abnormality test and the borrowing ceiling punish a figure chosen to look minimal.

## When the capital question disappears entirely

**The importer-of-record route is an arrangement in which a licensed trading company imports and holds title in its own name on your behalf, so no mainland entity needs to be capitalised at all.** For a brand testing China, this removes the five-year clock, the acceleration risk and the repatriation arithmetic in one move: you sell to the importer, and the importer carries the registration, the customs exposure and the inventory. What you give up is direct control of the legal entity and the option to hold licences in your own name.

Most foreign brands run the sequence in that order — importer-of-record first, WFOE once volume justifies the fixed cost — so the capital figure becomes a live decision only at the second step.
