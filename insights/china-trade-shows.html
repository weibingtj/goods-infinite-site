China Trade Shows for Foreign Brands: Canton Fair, CIIE and How to Exhibit

Exhibiting in China is two separate legal events: getting your people and your goods into the country, then deciding what happens to the goods afterwards. This guide maps the four national shows worth a foreign brand's budget, the Canton Fair International Pavilion application, how ATA carnets and temporary-import rules keep your samples duty-free, what an M visa invitation letter must contain, and why selling off the stand is the step that catches most first-time exhibitors.

Published 2026-10-14 · Last updated 2026-10-16 · By Bing Wei, China Market Entry & Cross-Border Commerce Specialist

Exhibiting in China means one thing above all: two separate legal events — your people and your goods — plus a third decision about what happens when the show ends

A China trade show is not a marketing booking; it is a temporary import operation, a visa exercise and a distribution decision stacked on top of each other. Most foreign brands plan the booth and discover the customs question in week two. The correct order is the opposite: the goods rule decides what you can physically bring, the visa rule decides who can stand behind the booth, and the post-show rule decides whether a conversation on the stand can turn into a delivered order.

That framing answers the question behind "which are the best trade shows in China for foreign brands?" The answer is not one show. It is four national-level fairs with four different buyers, plus a decision about which one matches the channel you intend to build.

The stand is the cheapest part. The expensive part is what you do with the goods when the lights go out.

Two practical notes. First, all four are government-hosted national exhibitions, which is why they come with on-site customs, IP and trade-dispute services that commercial shows do not have. Second, the fair that is right for you is decided by the buyer you need: sourcing and distribution partners across the whole country points to the Canton Fair; Chinese importers and platform buyers for a finished consumer product points to CIIE or CICPE.

Can a foreign company actually exhibit — and what does the application require?

Yes, and no Chinese entity is required to stand on the floor. The Canton Fair's International Pavilion admission rules are explicit: the exhibitor must be a company legally registered outside mainland China, and must provide a copy of its certificate of incorporation bearing the company seal, or other valid proof. Group companies, subsidiaries, joint-venture partners and representative offices with a substantive legal relationship to the applicant may co-exhibit, provided supporting documents and authorisation are filed. Exhibits must fall within the published International Pavilion product scope.

The application pack is where first-timers lose weeks. Expect to produce:

  1. A company registration certificate (business licence equivalent) plus corporate and product introductions.
  2. Evidence connecting you to the brand — proof of origin, overseas brand registration, proof of the relationship with the overseas brand owner, or an exhibition authorisation.
  3. The completed application form, exhibition terms and online-exhibition responsibility letter, each company-sealed.
  4. Payment of the participation fee after the organiser issues the payment notice — payment in full is what confirms the booth.
  5. Registration on the exhibitor system (参展易捷通) for first-time exhibitors.

Applications can be filed directly with the China Foreign Trade Centre or through contracted overseas recruiters, and the organiser warns that the official exhibitor system is the only legitimate channel. Note the contrast with selling online: a foreign brand can exhibit without a mainland entity, but holding a domestic storefront on a Chinese B2B marketplace requires a mainland-registered business — the distinction is set out in selling on 1688 as a foreign brand.

Getting the goods in: temporary import, ATA carnets and the six-month clock

Exhibition goods are a defined customs category, not an awkward edge case. Article 3 of GACC Order No. 233 (Administrative Measures for the Temporary Import and Export of Goods, published 7 December 2017, in force 1 February 2018, as amended by GACC Order No. 273 of 28 October 2024) lists, as its first category, goods displayed or used at exhibitions, trade fairs, conferences and similar activities.

Three consequences follow, and they are the ones that matter operationally:

  • Duty is deferred, not waived. Article 37 of the Customs Tariff Law (in force 1 December 2024) provides that goods temporarily imported for display or use at exhibitions, fairs and conferences may lawfully defer customs duty, provided they are re-exported within six months of importation, with extensions handled under GACC rules. Goods not re-exported within the period must pay duty.
  • Licences are generally not required. Article 5 of Order No. 233 exempts temporary import/export goods from licence presentation, except where a treaty, law, administrative regulation or GACC rule provides otherwise. This is why a product that would normally need a registration certificate can still be shown — but note that showing is not selling; the registration chain resumes the moment you place the product on the market, as mapped in which Chinese agency regulates my product.
  • The clock is real. Article 10 of Order No. 233 gives six months for re-export, with extensions up to three times of no more than six months each. Article 13 requires the formal import or export declaration to be completed before that deadline if the goods are not leaving.

ATA carnet or ordinary temporary import?

An ATA carnet is the "passport for goods" for temporary admission, and in China the issuing and guaranteeing authority is the China Council for the Promotion of International Trade (CCPIT / China Chamber of International Commerce), authorised by the State Council and GACC. Article 9 of Order No. 233 confirms that for carnet-covered goods, CCPIT provides a general guarantee to GACC — which is the practical reason a carnet holder does not post a bond at the border, while a non-carnet temporary import does.

The carnet's limits are exactly where exhibitors get caught: it covers commercial samples, exhibition goods and professional equipment; it does not cover perishables or consumables, goods imported for the purpose of sale, goods intended as gifts, or goods imported for processing. It is valid for one year and can be used across multiple countries and multiple entries. Applications run through CCPIT's ATA system (eatachina.com), and the applicant must be the goods owner or a person free to dispose of them.

Where a carnet is not appropriate — because you intend to leave stock behind, or the goods are consumable — customs offers alternative supervision codes: customs records exhibition filing, and goods can clear as 2600 (temporary import/export goods), 2700 (exhibition goods), an ATA carnet, or ordinary trade 0110 if they are being imported properly. Small samples and consumables distributed free to visitors during the fair can be relieved of import duty and import-stage taxes within a reasonable quantity and total value that customs assesses against the nature of the exhibition, the exhibitor's scale and visitor numbers.

Getting your people in: the M visa and the invitation letter

Attending a fair is commercial activity, so the correct category is the M visa. Under Article 7(8) of the Regulations on the Administration of the Entry and Exit of Foreigners (as amended in 2025), an applicant for an M visa must submit an invitation letter issued by the Chinese commercial or trade partner.

The Chinese Visa Application Service Centre's M-visa material list specifies what that letter must contain:

  • Invitee details — name, gender, date of birth, passport number, etc.
  • Visit details — purpose of the visit, arrival and departure dates, places to be visited, the relationship with the inviting entity, and the source of funds for the stay.
  • Inviting entity details — name, address and telephone, the entity's official seal, and the signature of its legal representative (or of the inviting person, with their position stated), plus the date of invitation.
  • Inviting entity's business licence, and a note in the letter if a multiple-entry visa is requested.

In practice the invitation can come from the Chinese counterparty you are meeting, the fair organiser's overseas recruiter, or the mainland service provider running your China-side programme. For genuinely urgent business travel — a last-minute stand problem, a signing — port visas in the trade (M) category are available under the National Immigration Administration's facilitation measures, on an invitation letter from an authorised or registered inviting entity.

Selling from the stand: the step that surprises first-time exhibitors

This is where the temporary-import logic and the commercial logic collide, and it is worth stating plainly: if the goods will be sold, they are not temporary-import goods.

  • Under the ATA carnet system, goods imported for the purpose of sale are excluded. If you sell carnet goods in China and they never leave, the guaranteeing chamber is exposed to the import taxes, and the holder is on the hook.
  • Under Order No. 233, goods that will not be re-exported must complete a normal import declaration before the deadline (Art. 13). That means a Chinese importer of record, classification, duty and import VAT — the same chain any commercial shipment follows, set out in importing into China without a local company.
  • Once goods move from the stand into commerce, labelling and product-compliance obligations attach — Chinese-language labelling is a market-placement requirement, not a display requirement, and the details are in China import product labelling requirements.

The one structural exception is CIIE. A joint notice of the Ministry of Finance, GACC and the State Taxation Administration (Cai Guan Shui [2020] No. 38) grants exemption from import duty, import-stage VAT and consumption tax on a reasonable quantity of imported exhibits sold during the CIIE exhibition period, excluding prohibited imports, endangered species products, tobacco, alcohol, automobiles and products on the catalogue of major technical equipment not eligible for duty exemption. Per-exhibitor relief is capped: listed categories 1–5 by quantity, and US$20,000 for other exhibits. Exhibits sold above the limit, and exhibits unsold during the fair that are not re-exported afterwards, are taxed under the ordinary rules. Confirm the current session's policy with the organiser before you plan a sell-through.

Intellectual property: the fair has its own enforcement system

China's national fairs run on-site IP machinery, and it is fast. At the Canton Fair, intellectual property and trade dispute reception stations are staffed during the exhibition; on receipt of a complaint the exhibitor is notified and has 24 hours to choose one of two paths — voluntarily remove the complained-of exhibit, in which case no infringement determination is made and the dispute ends, or file a defence with evidence through the system, in which case the station investigates and determines whether infringement is indicated. Determinations and sanctions follow the fair's own measures on complaints of suspected IP infringement, and repeat display of an item already found to be complained of, or display of items already adjudged infringing by a court or administrative decision, is treated as an aggravating factor. The expert teams are drawn from national, provincial and municipal IP administrations.

The defensive work happens before you ship: China is a first-to-file jurisdiction, and exhibiting an unregistered brand is exactly the scenario where that bites — see trademark registration for foreign brands in China.

After the show: converting a stand into a channel

A fair produces conversations, not sales. Turning them into revenue means picking the import route that matches what you promised on the stand:

  1. Cross-border e-commerce (1210 bonded) — fast, low-commitment, keeps inventory in a bonded zone until an order arrives, ideal for validating demand with real orders. The mechanics are in what is 1210 bonded import.
  2. General trade import — the route for shelf distribution, retail and B2B invoicing in RMB; requires the full compliance set and a mainland importer.
  3. A distributor or agent — reach without your own entity, with the control trade-offs set out in finding a China distributor.
  4. Your own China presence — an office, staff and a mandate, which is what appointing a China commercial representative is for.

Before you commit to any of them, pressure-test what you heard on the stand with structured research rather than booth enthusiasm — the method is in China market research for foreign brands.

FAQ

Can a foreign brand exhibit at the Canton Fair without a Chinese entity? Yes — the International Pavilion exists precisely for that. The exhibitor must be a company legally registered outside mainland China, must provide a sealed copy of its certificate of incorporation, and must produce evidence connecting it to the brand: proof of origin, overseas brand registration, proof of the relationship with the brand owner, or an exhibition authorisation. Applications go to the China Foreign Trade Centre or a contracted recruiter, and exhibits must be within the published product scope. Selling domestically at scale, issuing Chinese VAT invoices and acting as importer of record still require a mainland entity.

Do I pay customs duty on goods I bring into China for a trade show? Not if they leave again. Article 37 of the Customs Tariff Law (in force 1 December 2024) allows goods temporarily imported for display or use at exhibitions, fairs and conferences to defer duty, provided they are re-exported within six months. Temporary import/export goods are also exempt from licence presentation under Article 5 of GACC Order No. 233 unless an exception applies. Small samples distributed free to visitors during the fair can be relieved of import duty and import-stage taxes within a reasonable quantity and value assessed by customs.

How long can exhibition goods stay in China? Six months from importation or exportation under Article 10 of GACC Order No. 233, extendable up to three times for no more than six months each. When the extension expires, the goods must be re-exported, re-imported, or converted to a normal declaration. Goods for exhibitions running 24 months or longer may be granted further extension by the competent directly-affiliated customs house.

Can I sell my samples or products from the booth? Not under an ATA carnet — goods imported for the purpose of sale are excluded from the carnet system. To sell, the goods must be moved onto a normal import declaration with a Chinese importer of record, paying duty and import VAT before the temporary-import deadline (Order No. 233, Art. 13), and market-placement requirements such as Chinese labelling then apply. The exception is the CIIE, where Cai Guan Shui [2020] No. 38 relieves import duty, import VAT and consumption tax on a reasonable quantity of imported exhibits sold during the fair, subject to per-exhibitor limits including a US$20,000 ceiling for unlisted categories.

What visa does my team need to attend a trade fair in China? An M visa. Article 7(8) of the Regulations on the Administration of the Entry and Exit of Foreigners requires an invitation letter from the Chinese commercial or trade partner. Per the Chinese Visa Application Service Centre checklist, the letter must state the invitee's personal details, the visit details (purpose, arrival and departure dates, places visited, relationship with the inviting entity, source of funds) and the inviting entity's details (name, address, telephone, official seal, signature of the legal representative), and the inviting entity's business licence is required. Urgent business travel can use an M-category port visa under the National Immigration Administration's facilitation measures.
