## China has no legal category called "supplement" — and that single fact decides your entire launch

Ask a Chinese regulator what a "supplement" is and you will get three different answers depending on which desk you ask. In Chinese law the word does not exist as a product category. What exists is a classification exercise: a bottle of vitamins, a fish-oil softgel and a collagen sachet each fall into one of three legal identities, and each identity comes with its own approval clock, its own claim ceiling and its own distribution rights. Get the classification right before you commission artwork and you can be selling in weeks. Get it wrong and the most common outcome is not a fine — it is a two-year pause while a registration dossier works its way through technical review, or a listing that gets pulled the week after launch because the copy implied a function the product is not approved to claim.

This guide sets out the decision tree, the statutory hooks behind each branch, and the numbers that actually drive the timeline.

## Ordinary food is the fastest route and the one that forbids the most

There is a widespread assumption among foreign supplement brands that entering as ordinary food is a loophole. It is not — it is a legally valid route that simply comes with a gag order attached.

Art. 38 of the <a href="https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/content/2019-10/31/content_5447142.htm" rel="nofollow">Regulation on the Implementation of the Food Safety Law</a> (State Council Order No. 721, in force 1 December 2019) is one sentence long and does all the work: **for foods other than health food, no claim of a health function may be made.** Art. 39 then requires sellers to check that the label and instructions match the registered or filed version before selling, and prohibits displaying special foods mixed with ordinary food or with drugs.

In practice this means a product that enters as ordinary food can be imported, bonded, listed and sold — but the words "immune", "sleep", "joint", "liver", "blood sugar" and their softer cousins ("supports", "helps maintain", "clinically studied") all have to come off the label, the detail page and the livestream script. Most enforcement actions in this category are claim actions, not import actions: the goods cleared customs perfectly well and were then sold with copy that the product's legal identity does not permit.

## Health food: the blue hat, and the two routes that get you one

Health food (保健食品) is a statutory special-food category — defined in GB 16740 as food that claims and has a specific health function, or that is intended to supplement vitamins and minerals, is suitable for a defined population, regulates body function, is not intended to treat disease, and produces no acute, sub-acute or chronic harm. Approved products carry the blue hat-shaped mark plus an approval number. Only those products may make a health function claim.

The <a href="https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/2020-11/03/content_5723694.htm" rel="nofollow">Measures for the Administration of Health Food Registration and Filing</a> (published as CFDA Order No. 22, revised by SAMR Order No. 31, in force 23 October 2020) split the route in two.

### Filing (备案): directory ingredients, on-the-spot completion

Art. 9 requires registration for products using an ingredient **outside** the health food raw material directory, and for **first-import** health foods — with one carve-out: a first-import health food that is a vitamin/mineral nutrient supplement is filed instead of registered. Art. 5 assigns the filing of first-import nutrient supplements to SAMR itself, and other filings to the provincial market regulator. Art. 50 states that where the filing materials are satisfactory, filing is completed **on the spot**, and Art. 51 sets the numbering format 食健备G (domestic) / 食健备J (imported).

The current directory, per SAMR's own 2026 reply, covers **24 nutrients corresponding to 85 compound sources** and **10 non-nutrient ingredients** (ginseng, American ginseng, lingzhi and others). A parallel SAMR paper on marine health food confirms that ten functional ingredients including fish oil and spirulina have been brought into the filing directory.

Art. 27 is the one that catches people: **on-site verification, re-testing and re-review time are not counted** in the review or decision periods. So the statutory 20 working days at SAMR sits on top of a technical review that can run 80 working days and restart.

Two further points decide whether you can file at all.

**Who may apply.** Art. 11: the applicant for an imported health food registration must be the **overseas manufacturer** that markets the product, and the application must be handled through its resident representative office in China or a **commissioned Chinese agency**. A foreign trading company that does not manufacture cannot be the applicant.

**The origin-market gate.** Art. 13 requires, for a first-import registration, proof that the product **has been marketed for more than one year** in the country of origin, or a safety report covering overseas sales and human consumption; the relevant technical regulations or standards of that country; and actual samples of the overseas package, label and instructions. Alongside this, Art. 76 of the <a href="https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/2015-04/25/content_2853643.htm" rel="nofollow">Food Safety Law</a> requires that an imported health food be a product permitted to be marketed by the competent authority of the exporting country or region. A brand-new SKU launched only in the home market last quarter cannot be registered in China next quarter.

For nutrient supplements, the separate 2023 catalogue permits supplement claims for 24 nutrients and n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids, and requires the explanatory wording to follow Annex D of GB 28050 with **no deletion, addition or merging** of the standard claim wording.

The catalogue is also being extended in the background. Under SAMR document Shi Jian Te Shi Fa [2025] No. 82, a pilot on filing of domestically produced compound-formula health food has brought 36 traditional Chinese medicinal materials — including dendrobium, gastrodia, polygonatum and astragalus — into a pilot scope. That pilot concerns domestic compound-formula products; it does not open a shortcut for imported single-ingredient SKUs today, but it signals the direction of travel.

Claims are then policed on the marketing side. Art. 78 of the Food Safety Law requires the label and instructions of a health food to avoid any disease-prevention or treatment function, to be consistent with the registered or filed content, to state the suitable and unsuitable populations and the functional component and its amount, and to carry the declaration **"this product cannot replace medicine"**. Under the <a href="https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/content/2020/content_5488918.htm" rel="nofollow">Measures for the Administration of Advertising Review of Drugs, Medical Devices, Health Foods and Foods for Special Medical Purposes</a> (SAMR Order No. 21, in force 1 March 2020), Art. 2 prohibits publishing a health food advertisement **without prior review**, and Art. 7 requires the advertisement to follow the registration certificate or filing record and product instructions, to avoid disease prevention and treatment functions, and to state prominently that a health food is not a drug and cannot replace drug treatment.

## The cross-border route: exempt from first-import approval, not from everything else

For most supplement brands this is the route that actually gets revenue started, and it is often misunderstood as a deregulated channel. It is not deregulated — it is *differently* regulated.

Section 3 of the Circular on Improving Supervision of Cross-Border E-Commerce Retail Imports (<a href="https://www.mofcom.gov.cn/zfxxgk/gkml/art/2022/art_c44f2c0b53884baa8d07ecc5a2796325.html" rel="nofollow">Shang Cai Fa [2018] No. 486</a>, six departments including MOFCOM and GACC) provides that goods imported through the cross-border retail import channel are supervised as **articles for personal use** and are **not subject to the first-import licence, registration or filing requirements** applicable to goods — with two exceptions: goods from epidemic areas whose import has been suspended, and goods under a major quality-safety risk response. GACC's own published answers and the customs service guides restate this for food specifically: cross-border imported food is supervised as an article for personal use, no first-import licence, registration or filing is required, and the goods may arrive without Chinese labelling because they are assessed against the origin market's standards.

Three conditions still apply, and they are hard conditions:

1. **The commodity must be on the positive list.** The Cross-Border E-Commerce Retail Import Commodity List is maintained by the finance, customs and commerce authorities; GACC's January 2026 policy briefing states that the most recent amending notice is 2022 No. 7, covering 1,476 tariff lines, and that the list is adjusted dynamically. If your HS code is not on it, there is no cross-border route.
2. **Per-order and annual caps.** The 12360 hotline answers published in August 2026 confirm the limits in force: RMB 5,000 per transaction and RMB 26,000 per person per year. Figures circulating in the trade press for higher caps are not supported by an official document we can cite, and we do not repeat them.
3. **Responsibilities do not disappear.** Under the same circular, the cross-border e-commerce enterprise bears primary responsibility for quality safety and consumer protection, must entrust an enterprise registered in China and bear joint and several civil liability, and the platform must operate a risk-control mechanism and honour advance compensation. Consumers themselves need no qualification — a point GACC's January 2026 briefing makes explicitly for health products, cosmetics and infant formula.

What cross-border does **not** give you is a claim. Nothing in the circular authorises a health-function claim for a product that is not a registered or filed health food, and nothing exempts the listing copy from China's advertising law. It also does not open offline distribution: goods bought through the channel are for personal use and may not be resold into the domestic market.

## One requirement that sits outside the blue hat entirely: GACC manufacturer registration

Health food is food. For general trade import, the overseas manufacturing establishment is therefore within the scope of **GACC Decree 248**, the registration regime for overseas food manufacturers that has applied since 1 January 2022, administered by the <a href="http://www.customs.gov.cn" rel="nofollow">General Administration of Customs</a>. A blue-hat certificate and a registered factory are independent requirements: holding one does not substitute for the other.

Where the two overlap with the cross-border channel, we flag rather than assert: Shang Cai Fa [2018] No. 486 exempts the *goods* from first-import registration and filing, while Decree 248 registers the *establishment*. We have not identified an official document settling how the two interact for every HS code and bonded model, so confirm this per SKU with your customs broker before you commit stock.

## A working sequence for a supplement brand starting from zero

The order below is the one that avoids the two expensive failure modes — waiting two years with no revenue, and launching claims that have to be pulled.

1. **Strip the claims and classify.** Take every SKU and list its ingredients, dosages and the claim you want. If the claim survives the strip test, you do not need the blue hat yet; if the claim is the product, you do.
2. **Check the raw material directory first.** Directory ingredient at directory dosage with the matching directory function points at filing. Anything else points at registration.
3. **Check the origin-market gate.** For a first-import registration you need one year of marketing in the country of origin, or a documented safety report. If the SKU is too new, launch it as ordinary food or cross-border and come back later.
4. **Register the trademark before you talk to anyone.** China is a first-to-file system and distributor conversations are where marks get taken.
5. **Launch cross-border** on a positive-list HS code, with claim-free copy, to build real demand data.
6. **Run the filing or registration track in parallel**, funded by that revenue, with a Chinese agency appointed before the dossier is lodged.
7. **Fix artwork once, at the end.** Label, insert and listing copy must match the registered or filed text exactly — Art. 39 of the Implementation Regulation makes a mismatch unsellable.
8. **Add general trade** only when the certificate is in hand and the factory is GACC-registered. That is when offline pharmacy and supermarket become available.

## What we deliberately do not assert

We would rather leave a gap than invent a number. Specifically:

- **No timeline estimate for a registration in total months.** The statutory working-day clocks are published; dossier preparation, translation and China-recognised laboratory testing are not, and any figure we gave would be our guess rather than a regulator's.
- **No fee schedule.** Neither registration nor filing has a published official fee schedule we can cite, and third-party quotes vary widely.
- **No GACC/486 interaction ruling** for cross-border consignments, as set out above.
- **No claim that cross-border authorises a health claim.** It does not, and there is no official text saying otherwise.
- **No enforcement case studies.** Penalty figures for claim violations depend on the specific provision applied and the advertising spend involved; we have not found an official text we can cite for a specific supplement case and so we quote none.
- **No statement that the 24-function catalogue is frozen.** It is maintained under the <a href="https://cfe-samr.org.cn/zcfg/bjsp_134/bmgz/202207/t20220729_4386.html" rel="nofollow">Measures for the Administration of Health Food Raw Material and Function Claim Catalogues</a> (SAMR Order No. 13, in force 1 October 2019), which provides for re-evaluation and adjustment, and a draft rule on probiotic health food was put out for public comment in July 2026 but has not been promulgated.

## FAQ

**Is a "supplement" a legal product category in China?** No. Chinese food law has no such category. Each product is either health food (requiring registration or filing and carrying the blue hat), ordinary food (sellable but silent on function), or a cross-border retail import supervised as an article for personal use. Ingredients, dosage and intended claim decide which.

**Can I sell a supplement in China as ordinary food without the blue hat?** You can sell it, but you may claim nothing. Art. 38 of the Implementation Regulation prohibits health-function claims on foods that are not health food, and Art. 39 requires the label to match the registered or filed text and forbids mixing special foods with ordinary food or drugs on the shelf.

**What is the difference between health food registration and filing?** Filing applies to directory ingredients at directory dosage with the matching directory function, and is completed on the spot where the dossier is complete. Registration applies to non-directory ingredients and to first-import health foods that are not vitamin/mineral supplements, and runs a substantive technical review of 60 working days extendable by 20, with verification and re-testing time excluded.

**How many health claims are allowed?** Twenty-four non-nutrient functions under the 2023 catalogue, plus the nutrient-supplement catalogue's 24 nutrients and n-3. Any claim outside those lists is prohibited on a food product, and the nutrient wording must follow GB 28050 Annex D without editing.

**Can I sell into China through cross-border e-commerce without a blue hat?** Generally yes. Section 3 of Shang Cai Fa [2018] No. 486 exempts cross-border retail imports from first-import licence, registration and filing, subject to the positive list (1,476 tariff lines under the 2022 No. 7 notice) and the RMB 5,000 / RMB 26,000 caps. The channel grants no claim and no offline distribution.

**Who can apply for registration?** The overseas manufacturer only, acting through a resident representative office in China or a commissioned Chinese agency, and for a first import it must produce proof of at least one year of marketing in the origin market or an overseas safety report, plus overseas package, label and instruction samples.

**Does the cross-border exemption remove GACC manufacturer registration too?** We do not assert either answer. The circular exempts the goods from first-import approval; Decree 248 registers the establishment. Confirm per HS code and channel with your broker.
