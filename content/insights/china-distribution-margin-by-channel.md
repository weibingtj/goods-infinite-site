## What a distribution margin is — and why it decides your China price

A China distribution margin is the share of the final selling price that each intermediary between your factory and the Chinese consumer keeps for itself, and the stack you choose determines what your product must retail for before it earns anything. A brand that ships at one dollar FOB cannot assume it will sell at three: in general trade, freight, duty, an importer-distributor, sometimes a sub-distributor and a retailer each take a defined cut, and the retail price is usually worked backwards from what the consumer will pay. New Zealand Trade and Enterprise's official China market guide is the cleanest public benchmark for the question every brand asks first.

## What distributors actually earn, by brand tier

Distributor margins in China scale inversely with brand power: the stronger your brand, the less of the stack you concede. Brokerage research on the Chinese FMCG trade shows international brands such as Coca-Cola, P&G and Wrigley giving distributors gross margins of only about 6 to 10 percent — the brand pays most route costs itself and the distributor earns on delivery coverage — while domestic second- and third-tier manufacturers, which need the distributor to carry the brand, concede 20 to 35 percent plus sales rebates of 3 to 10 percent ([Guorong Securities channel research, 2018](http://pdf.dfcfw.com/pdf/H2_AN201802011085210859_1.pdf)).

Category heat moves the number too: Beijing Daily's market reporting found imported food carrying wholesale gross margins of 35 to 50 percent against 10 to 15 percent for domestic brands ([Beijing Daily via The Paper](https://m.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_23993227)). The lesson is uncomfortable but useful — a distributor demanding the top of the range is telling you it does not believe your brand pulls demand, and the negotiation answer is structure, not surrender. ## What cross-border e-commerce takes instead

Cross-border e-commerce replaces the distribution stack with a platform commission, a cross-border tax rate and a fulfilment fee — and hands the marketing job back to you. The platform's cut is far smaller than a distributor's: Tmall Global's commission runs 2 to 5 percent by leaf category on top of a deposit and an annual technical service fee, and third-party consultancy analysis of JD Worldwide reports category commissions of roughly 0.5 to 6 percent plus a 0.6 percent payment settlement fee ([PLTFRM, JD Worldwide cost structure 2025](https://pltfrm.com.cn/?p=60461)).

But the stack changes shape rather than disappearing. Cross-border retail imports pay duty of zero with VAT and consumption tax at 70 percent of the statutory rate — 9.1 percent effective on a 13 percent VAT good, within the RMB 5,000 per order and RMB 26,000 per year limits ([GACC policy briefing](http://www.customs.gov.cn/customs/2026-01/13/article_2026041015241860378.html)) — plus bonded fulfilment and the full marketing budget. An operator adds more: a Tmall Global TP typically charges a retainer plus 5 to 15 percent of GMV (see our [TP partner guide](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/tmall-global-tp-partner.html)), and livestream agencies another 15 to 30 percent. The honest comparison: cross-border retains more gross margin and consumes it in demand creation, while general trade converts retained margin into channel partners who fund their own growth. ## Why one product carries several prices in China

Multi-channel pricing in China is the practice of holding one product at different net prices per channel so that every intermediary can earn its expected margin at a single agreed retail price, and it is a discipline, not a trick. The offline chain needs room for a 25 to 40 percent retail margin; a membership club wants a lower shelf price and a higher volume commitment; your own cross-border store can undercut the offline shelf — and if it does, you will be asked to raise the online price or lose the listing.

Chinese rules actually support disciplined pricing. Under the retail supplier fairness rules, a retailer may not restrict the prices a supplier charges other operators or dictate the supplier's prices to consumers — resale price maintenance is prohibited (Article 7, MOFCOM and four agencies' Order No. 17 of 2006), though agreed promotion and rebate mechanics remain lawful ([full text, MOFCOM](https://sczxs.mofcom.gov.cn/article/cbw/ddfg/ag/201208/20120808294156.shtml)). Build the price ladder before the first distributor conversation: fix the target retail price, subtract each layer, and check that what remains funds a distributor at a market rate. ## How to protect your margin in distributor negotiations

Margin protection is won through contract structure rather than the headline discount, and four mechanisms do most of the work. Quote an ex-warehouse supply price and pay for growth through performance rebates of 3 to 10 percent against agreed volume and listing targets, instead of a fat list discount that becomes the floor of every future negotiation. Fix payment terms inside the legal frame: payment periods may not exceed 60 days from receipt of goods under the same rules, and the 2026 domestic-trade transaction guidelines encourage payment within 30 days with overdue interest referenced to the one-year LPR — late payment is silent margin erosion, so model cash conversion, not just the gross point. Price exclusivity separately, because national exclusivity without investment commitments is the most expensive concession a brand gives away. Finally, audit the distributor's proposed retail price against your price ladder before signing; once the market price forms it is extremely hard to lift. ## FAQ

**What margin does a China distributor take on imported goods?** For general trade, an importer-distributor margin of roughly 20 percent of sales is the working benchmark; New Zealand Trade and Enterprise's China market guide adds a retailer margin of 25 to 40 percent of sales and, where a sub-distributor sits in the chain, about 15 percent more. The real number depends on brand power: brokerage research on China FMCG shows international brands conceding only 6 to 10 percent to distributors, while domestic second- and third-tier brands hand over 20 to 35 percent.

**How much margin does the retailer take in China?** The retailer's share is the largest layer of the general-trade stack: 25 to 40 percent of sales depending on the channel, with imported food and personal care often carrying wholesale gross margins of 35 to 50 percent — far above the 10 to 15 percent typical for domestic brands. On top of margin, large retailers negotiate annual rebates, listing support and promotional funding, so model the net effective margin, not just the gross point.

**Is cross-border e-commerce cheaper for a foreign brand than using a distributor?** The platform itself takes far less — Tmall Global's commission is 2 to 5 percent and JD Worldwide's category commission is reported at roughly 0.5 to 6 percent, against a distributor's 20 percent — but cross-border replaces margin with marketing. The brand funds demand creation itself, typically the largest recurring line, plus bonded fulfilment and the cross-border tax of 9.1 percent effective on a 13 percent VAT good. Margin retained is higher, but so is the cash and operating burden.

**Why does the same imported product sell at different prices in different China channels?** Each channel has its own stack. A general-trade chain adds importer margin, sub-distributor margin and 25 to 40 percent retail margin on top of duty, VAT and logistics; a bonded cross-border store adds only platform commission, tax and fulfilment; a membership club or livestream session has its own terms. Brands therefore build a price ladder that fixes the retail price first and works backwards, so the same product can sit in every channel without one undercutting another.

**How can a foreign brand protect its margin when appointing a China distributor?** Quote an ex-warehouse supply price and pay growth through performance rebates instead of a fat list discount; keep contract terms inside the legal frame — retailers may not dictate your resale prices and payment terms cannot exceed 60 days from receipt of goods under the retail supplier fairness rules; price exclusivity separately rather than giving it away; and audit the distributor's proposed retail price against your price ladder before signing, because once the market price forms it is extremely hard to lift.
