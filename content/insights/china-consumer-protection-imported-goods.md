  - q: Does China's 7-day no-reason return rule apply to imported goods?
    a: Yes for goods sold to Chinese consumers by network, TV, telephone or mail order. Art. 25 of the Consumer Protection Law draws no distinction by the origin of the goods, so an imported SKU sold on a domestic Chinese channel carries the same 7-day no-reason right as a local one. The separate question is goods cleared under the 1210 or 9610 cross-border retail import codes: neither the Consumer Protection Law, Decree No. 778, Order No. 90 nor the six-ministry Notice 486 of 2018 expressly settles how the 7-day right applies to that channel, so in practice it is governed by the platform rules and by the confirmation the consumer gives at purchase, not by an automatic statutory exemption.
  - q: Which goods are excluded from China's 7-day no-reason return?
    a: Four classes are excluded outright by Art. 25 of the Consumer Protection Law and Art. 6 of Order No. 90: custom-made goods, fresh or perishable goods, downloaded or unsealed digital media, and delivered newspapers or periodicals. Three further classes may be excluded only if the seller flags them prominently and the consumer confirms at the time of purchase: goods whose unsealing affects safety or health or changes quality, goods that lose substantial value once activated or tried, and near-expiry or defective goods disclosed at sale. Art. 19 of Decree No. 778 bars sellers from enlarging that list or making the exclusion a default ticked option.
  - q: What is the difference between a defective-goods return and a no-reason return in China?
    a: Art. 24 covers goods that fail to meet quality requirements: the consumer may return them or demand replacement or repair, and the operator bears the necessary transport costs. Art. 25 covers a change of mind with no reason required: the consumer bears the return freight unless otherwise agreed. Under Art. 10 of Order No. 90 the 7-day period starts the day after the consumer signs for the goods, and under Art. 13 the seller must refund within 7 days of receiving the returned goods.
  - q: What did State Council Decree No. 778 change for foreign brands selling into China?
    a: In force since 1 July 2024, it makes product-page and advertising promises about after-sales and liability binding (Art. 12), requires the seller's real name on the homepage and the name, business address and contact details of any other operator actually supplying the goods (Art. 13), sets a floor and a start date for repair/replacement/return terms (Art. 18), forbids default opt-outs from no-reason returns (Art. 19), requires a complaint mechanism (Art. 44), and gives regulators 7 working days to process a complaint and 60 days to complete mediation (Art. 46). Art. 50 sets penalties of one to five times illegal gain, or up to RMB 300,000 where there is no illegal gain.
  - q: Who is liable to a Chinese consumer when the seller is an overseas company?
    a: Under Notice 486 of 2018, the cross-border e-commerce enterprise — the overseas registered company that owns the goods — bears primary responsibility for product quality and safety and for consumer rights, including information disclosure, return and exchange services, a recall system and compensation. It must entrust a mainland-registered enterprise that registers with customs, declares truthfully and bears joint and several civil liability. The platform must operate a dispute system and perform advance payment to the consumer. This is why a foreign brand cannot leave consumer liability sitting with an entity nobody in China can reach.
  - q: Can a shopper return cross-border goods and get the import tax and annual purchase quota back?
    a: Not by simply mailing the parcel back. Under GACC Announcement 2020 No. 45, the mainland agent of the cross-border e-commerce enterprise, or the customs broker it entrusts, applies to customs for the return. Where the application is made within 30 days of release of the import declaration list and the goods are returned in their original state within 45 days to the original customs supervision site, the original special customs supervision area or a type-B bonded logistics centre, the corresponding tax is not levied and the consumer's annual cumulative purchase amount is adjusted.

## China's consumer-protection rules for imported goods are three separate legal routes, not one return policy — and which one applies is decided by how the goods were cleared, not by where the brand is incorporated

A foreign brand selling into China usually asks whether its goods "have to be accepted back". The answer is not a policy setting in a storefront. It is a legal chain: the [Consumer Protection Law](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/2013-10/25/content_2602290.htm) and the [Implementing Regulation, State Council Decree No. 778](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/content/202403/content_6940158.htm) define the seller's duties toward a Chinese consumer; the [Interim Measures on 7-Day No-Reason Returns, Order No. 90](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/2020-11/03/content_5723721.htm) define the procedure and the intact-goods test; and, where goods entered under the 1210 or 9610 cross-border retail codes, [Notice 486 of 2018](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2018-12/31/content_5437823.htm) and [GACC Announcement 2020 No. 45](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2020-03/28/content_5496571.htm) add a customs layer that most overseas sellers never see until a return goes wrong. None of these instruments exempts a product because it was imported. What changes is who stands behind the sale and what has to happen at the border.

The most expensive mistake is treating a defect claim as a change of mind, or treating a cross-border return as a normal parcel.

Two consequences follow. First, a defective imported batch cannot be pushed into the no-reason route to shift freight to the buyer. Second, the [customs return rules](http://www.customs.gov.cn/customs/fileDir/resource/cms/article/302267/2939263/2020033012514711531.doc) mean the commercial refund a platform shows the shopper is only half the transaction; the other half is a declaration-list procedure with a hard deadline.

## What the 7-day rule actually requires of a seller

Art. 25 grants the right, but Order No. 90 is where stores fail. It sets the exclusion list, defines when goods are still "intact", and imposes a purchase-flow confirmation step that cannot be hidden in terms and conditions.

Art. 20 of Order No. 90 requires the seller to build a conspicuous confirmation step into the sales flow for conditional-exclusion goods, and states that without it the seller may not refuse the return. Art. 19 of Decree No. 778 goes further: the exclusion may not be a default ticked option. Enforcement is not theoretical — Arts. 30 and 31 of Order No. 90 route an unjustified refusal, a failure to provide a valid return address within 15 days, and a failure to refund within 15 days of receiving the goods to the penalty in Art. 56(1)(8) of the Consumer Protection Law. In other words, a product page that simply says "no returns on imported goods" is not a policy; it is a violation.

## Decree No. 778: the 2024 implementing regulation most foreign brands have not mapped

Decree No. 778 (State Council Order No. 778, published 15 March 2024, in force 1 July 2024, seven chapters and 53 articles) does not create a new return right. It tightens the duties around it, and several of its articles land squarely on an overseas-brand-plus-mainland-operator structure.

For imported goods, Arts. 13 and 49 deserve a second read. Art. 13 is why a storefront cannot show only the overseas brand while a mainland company quietly handles declaration, warehousing and after-sales. Art. 49 is often quoted as a "labelling error is fine" rule — it is not: the defect must both leave quality unaffected <strong>and</strong> be non-misleading. Art. 51 preserves the ordinary Administrative Penalty Law routes to lighter or no punishment where the operator voluntarily removes the harm, or the breach is minor and promptly corrected.

## The cross-border exception: a 1210 return is not an ordinary e-commerce return

Notice 486 of 2018 defines four participants — the cross-border e-commerce enterprise (the overseas registered company that owns the goods), the mainland-registered platform, the mainland-registered service provider, and the consumer — and allocates liability in unusual detail. The overseas enterprise bears primary responsibility for quality and safety <strong>and</strong> for consumer rights, expressly including product information disclosure, return and exchange services, a recall system for defective goods, and compensation for quality-related harm. It must entrust one mainland-registered enterprise, which registers with customs, declares truthfully and bears joint and several civil liability. The platform must run a consumer-dispute system and perform advance payment. The consumer is the taxpayer, while the platform, logistics company or customs broker withholds and pays the tax and provides a tax guarantee to customs.

That last point is what makes the return different. Under [GACC Announcement 2020 No. 45](https://www.gov.cn/zhengce/zhengceku/2020-03/28/content_5496571.htm), the mainland agent or its broker (the "returning enterprise") may apply to customs for a return of all or part of the goods on the original declaration list. Where it applies within 30 days of that list's release and the goods are returned in their original state within 45 days to the original customs supervision site, the original special customs supervision area or a type-B bonded logistics centre, the corresponding tax is not levied and the consumer's annual cumulative purchase amount is adjusted. Where that customs sequence is not completed, the tax and quota outcome is a separate matter from any refund the platform may make under its advance-payment duty.

<div class="statcard"><span class="n">30 days / 45 days</span><span class="l">The two customs clocks behind a cross-border retail import return under GACC Announcement 2020 No. 45 — 30 days from release of the declaration list to apply, 45 days to get the goods back in original state to the original customs site, special supervision area or type-B bonded logistics centre</span><span class="src">Source: GACC Announcement 2020 No. 45, Arts. 1–3 (gov.cn and customs.gov.cn official text)</span></div>

Note also what the statutes do <strong>not</strong> say. The Consumer Protection Law, Decree No. 778, Order No. 90 and Notice 486 contain no provision that expressly resolves whether the 7-day no-reason right applies to goods cleared under 1210 or 9610. So the answer is not "imported goods are exempt" — it is set by the platform rules, by the risk notice the consumer must confirm before ordering, and by whether the seller has used the conditional-exclusion mechanism correctly. That is a design question for the listing and the returns workflow, not something to assume from the customs code. See our [cross-border returns guide](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/china-cross-border-ecommerce-returns.html) for the operational side, and [what 1210 bonded import is](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/what-is-1210-bonded-import.html) for the channel itself.

The practical implication is that return economics follow the bonded footprint. Because goods must come back into a customs-supervised site, a brand with stock in a bonded zone and a defined mainland entrustee can close the loop; a brand shipping direct-mail parcels with no mainland counterparty has no one able to file the customs return. This is the same structural gap we describe in [importing into China without a local company](https://www.goods-infinite.com/insights/importing-into-china-without-a-local-company.html).

## Two new rules from 1 February 2026

Two SAMR and Cyberspace Administration rules published on 18 December 2025 took effect on 1 February 2026, and both matter to a foreign brand using a TP partner or a livestream agency. [Order No. 116](https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2026/issue_12666/202604/content_7065115.html) governs how platform rules are made, amended and enforced: rules or their links must be displayed continuously in a prominent homepage position and be easy to read and download, and platforms must use those rules to implement product and service quality, consumer-rights, fair-competition, credit, personal-information and data responsibilities. [Order No. 117](https://www.gov.cn/gongbao/2026/issue_12666/202604/content_7065114.html) governs livestream commerce: it defines four participant categories, sets platform duties on identity verification and registration, information reporting, training, tiered management and transaction-record retention, requires live-room operators to display product names and prices, run pre-broadcast compliance reviews and keep correction and interaction records, and prohibits false or misleading commercial promotion. Both encourage first-contact responsibility, advance payment and online dispute resolution.

For an overseas brand, these duties usually sit with the Chinese TP or live-room operator. They should be written into the operating agreement, together with who answers the 12315 complaint trail and who keeps the chat, order and after-sales records. ## What we deliberately do not assert

- We do not give a single "three guarantees" (三包) term or a list of covered products. Art. 18 of Decree No. 778 sets a floor — an agreed term may not be shorter than the relevant state rules — and fixes when the clock starts and restarts; the concrete term depends on the product's applicable state rules and the contract.
- We do not assert that cross-border retail imports are categorically covered by, or categorically exempt from, the 7-day no-reason rule. No instrument we reviewed says so. We state the statutory rules and the customs sequence and leave the channel-specific application to the platform rules and the consumer's purchase-time confirmation.
- We do not quote return rates, return freight, compensation averages or any named enforcement case. No official source we could verify provides them for this question.
- We do not infer what happens to tax or quota when the 30/45-day customs sequence is missed. Art. 3 of Announcement 2020 No. 45 states the consequence for returns that meet both deadlines; it does not set out an exhaustive rule for all other cases.
- We do not claim that Notice 486's exemption from first-import licensing, registration or filing extends to consumer-protection obligations. The circular expressly assigns those obligations instead.

## FAQ

**Does the 7-day return apply to imported goods?** For goods sold on a domestic Chinese distance-selling channel, yes — Art. 25 draws no distinction by origin. For 1210/9610 cross-border retail imports, the statutes do not expressly settle it; platform rules and purchase-time confirmation decide.

**Which goods can be excluded?** Four statutory classes (custom-made, perishable, unsealed digital media, newspapers/periodicals) plus three classes that are excluded only after prominent marking and the consumer's confirmation at purchase.

**Who pays return freight?** The operator for a quality-defect return under Art. 24; the consumer for a no-reason return under Art. 25, unless agreed otherwise.

**What are the deadlines?** 7 days to notify a no-reason return from the day after signing, 7 days to refund after receiving the goods back, 7 working days for a regulator to process a complaint, 60 days to complete mediation, and 30/45 days for the customs-side cross-border return.

**Who is liable when the seller is overseas?** Under Notice 486 of 2018, the overseas e-commerce enterprise bears primary quality and consumer-rights responsibility, its mainland entrustee bears joint and several civil liability, and the platform performs advance payment.

**What are the penalties?** Decree No. 778 Art. 50 allows one to five times illegal gain or up to RMB 300,000 where there is none; Order No. 90 routes unjustified refusals and late refunds to Art. 56(1)(8) of the Consumer Protection Law.

**What changed in 2026?** Orders No. 116 and No. 117 took effect on 1 February 2026, tightening platform-rule transparency and livestream-commerce duties.
